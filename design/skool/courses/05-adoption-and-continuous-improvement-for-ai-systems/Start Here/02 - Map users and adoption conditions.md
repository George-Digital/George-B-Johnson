# Map users and adoption conditions

**People adopt a system when it fits a real moment of work, reduces a recognized burden, and gives them a safe way to question or decline its output.**

I do not treat “users” as one audience. The person who starts an AI-assisted workflow, the person who reviews it, the person who receives its output, and the person affected by the outcome may all have different needs. Mapping those roles before launch helps me avoid onboarding only the enthusiastic operator while leaving the reviewer, manager, or downstream recipient surprised by a new handoff.

I begin with the work moment. I ask where the user is when the system becomes relevant: receiving a request, preparing a weekly report, triaging a case, drafting a response, or checking a record before a meeting. Then I ask what they do now, what information is available, and what makes the current path frustrating. A system that requires a user to leave their normal workflow, collect unfamiliar data, or trust an unexplained result will need a stronger reason to be used.

I map adoption conditions alongside user roles. Conditions include access to the approved inputs, permission to use the system, enough time to run the process, a clear reason to choose it, and confidence that asking for help will not create blame. I also note local constraints: shifts, language, device access, disability accommodations, approval windows, existing forms, and union, client, or policy requirements. These are operating facts, not objections to work around.

I pay special attention to the reviewer. If an output arrives without sources, a stated confidence limit, or a way to see missing information, the reviewer inherits invisible risk. I design the output so the reviewer can inspect the basis for a claim and knows what still needs a human decision. For private material, I specify what users may enter, what they must exclude or anonymize, and where the approved system is available. Convenience does not override an organization’s privacy and access obligations.

I look for the hidden need behind resistance. A frontline user may not be resisting AI; they may be protecting a service standard, worried about being held responsible for a draft they did not create, or unable to find the current policy. A manager may need a simple way to know whether the process is used appropriately. A recipient may need consistent language and a way to correct an error. I turn these needs into design requirements rather than dismissing them as change aversion.

I do not force every user into the first pilot. I select an initial group with a real recurring use case, authority to try the process, and a route to provide candid feedback. I make participation expectations clear and keep a manual fallback available. When a user chooses the fallback, I want to know why. That choice may reveal a missing condition, a bad fit, or a necessary boundary.

By the end of this map, I should be able to explain not only who will touch the system, but what must be true for them to use it responsibly. This makes onboarding specific and lets the operating owner distinguish a training problem from a workflow or permission problem.

## Concrete example

For a small facilities team, a dispatcher starts an AI-assisted work-order summary, a supervisor reviews priority and assignment, technicians receive the final brief, and tenants are affected by the response. The dispatcher needs an approved request source and a fast way to mark missing details. The supervisor needs safety signals shown separately from a suggested summary. Technicians need the location and access notes, not the original private request history. The pilot includes dispatchers on the day shift and retains the existing manual triage path for urgent or unclear cases.

## Action checklist

- List the starter, reviewer, recipient, owner, and affected person for the workflow.
- Describe the work moment and current path for each primary user.
- Identify the access, permission, time, and information conditions for safe use.
- Record what each role needs to trust, inspect, or correct.
- State the approved privacy and data-entry boundary.
- Select a small initial user group and a manual fallback.

## Required deliverable

Create a **User and Adoption Map** that names each role, its work moment, current friction, adoption conditions, privacy or access constraints, review need, likely concern, and the fallback path.

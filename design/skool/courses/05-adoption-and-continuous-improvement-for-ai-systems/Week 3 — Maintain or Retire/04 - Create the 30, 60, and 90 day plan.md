# Create the 30, 60, and 90 day plan

**A 30-, 60-, and 90-day plan turns pilot evidence into a sequence of owned decisions instead of an open-ended promise to improve later.**

I use the next ninety days to decide whether the system deserves continued care, a bounded improvement, a narrower scope, a pause, or retirement. I do not make the plan a feature wishlist. Each period has an operating question, a small set of evidence, a named owner, and a decision point. This keeps the team focused on whether the process works for real people under its stated boundaries.

In the first thirty days, I stabilize the common path. I make sure users can find the current onboarding, SOP, support route, and manual fallback. I watch early use, first-run questions, review results, and any source or access issue. I fix only the clear, bounded problems that evidence supports, and I log each change. If a stop trigger appears, I pause the affected path rather than treating the first month as a period when problems should be tolerated.

By sixty days, I expect a clearer picture of adoption and quality. I compare the current scorecard with the baseline, while noting changes in case mix, staffing, source quality, or pilot scope. I review whether users understand fit conditions, whether reviewers can inspect outputs efficiently, and whether support patterns are declining or revealing a deeper design problem. I confirm that maintenance roles and backups are working in practice, not merely named in a document.

At ninety days, I hold a deliberate operating review. I bring the safe evidence: usage and quality trends, representative review records, fallbacks, feedback themes, source and dependency changes, support load, maintenance capacity, and unresolved risks. I ask whether the system still solves the stated problem, whether the human-review boundary remains adequate, and whether privacy and access controls are being followed. The output is a decision, not just a report.

The decision choices are explicit. We may maintain the current scope with ordinary review; improve one evidence-backed weakness; run a new bounded pilot for a changed use case; narrow the system to the cases it handles well; pause while a source, permission, or owner issue is resolved; or retire it and return users to a better process. I write the reason, authority, next step, and user communication for whichever choice we make.

I make the plan realistic about capacity. A system that requires weekly expert review may be valuable, but only if that review is available. If the owner cannot maintain sources, handle support, or run the agreed quality check, continued expansion is not responsible. The 90-day review is where I face that fact and decide whether to simplify, resource the work properly, or stop.

I share the decision in language users can act on. They need to know the current version, what has changed in their common path, what remains out of scope, where to ask questions, and what manual route applies during any pause. I do not bury a material change in an old log. Clear communication is part of adoption and part of retirement when that is the right decision.

## Concrete example

A museum team pilots a workflow that drafts exhibit-maintenance summaries from approved technician notes. In the first 30 days, the owner fixes an unclear source-date check and records several manual fallbacks for complex conservation issues. At 60 days, reviewers report that ordinary summaries are complete but require better links to source notes; the team adds those links and retests. At 90 days, the scorecard shows consistent use for routine maintenance, but the conservation-specialist review remains necessary for fragile artifacts. The team maintains the narrow routine scope, schedules quarterly source checks, and explicitly excludes conservation recommendations from the workflow.

## Action checklist

- Set a 30-day stabilization question and evidence review.
- Set a 60-day adoption, quality, and maintenance-capacity check.
- Set a 90-day maintain, improve, narrow, pause, or retire decision meeting.
- Assign an owner, evidence sources, and communication task for each point.
- Record changes in scope, sources, staffing, or case mix that affect comparison.
- Keep pause criteria and the manual fallback active throughout the plan.

## Required deliverable

Create a **30-, 60-, and 90-Day Operating Plan** with dates or event triggers, questions, evidence, accountable owners, review participants, allowed decisions, required communications, maintenance commitments, pause criteria, and the current manual fallback.

# Week 2: Measure the operating process and improve with evidence

**Embed on:** `Week 2 — Measure and Improve/01 - Select usage and quality signals.md`

**Estimated recording time:** 9–10 minutes

**Outcome:** Members will use a small, privacy-aware scorecard, feedback plan, review cadence, and change log to turn operating evidence into bounded decisions rather than relying on activity, opinions, or unrecorded edits.

## Teleprompter script

[ON SCREEN: Title card: “Week 2 — Measure and Improve”]

Hi, I’m George. This week, I am not trying to prove that an AI system is good because it is busy. I am building a small operating evidence loop: what people used, whether the output supported the next action, what burden it created, what people reported, what changed, and who decided what to do next.

[ON SCREEN: Synthetic label: “Harborlight Community Center event-request brief workflow — SYNTHETIC TRAINING EXAMPLE”]

I am still using our fictional Harborlight workflow. It prepares a draft event-request brief from a fictional approved form, and an Events Lead reviews it. Everything you see is synthetic. I am not using real member, client, employee, volunteer, or confidential information, and I do not recommend collecting it in general feedback or measurement tools.

[ON SCREEN: Open `Resources/Usage and Quality Scorecard.md`]

I start with a scorecard that serves a decision. Before I collect a number, I ask: what decision might this inform? In this case, the options are maintain the current defined scope, clarify onboarding, make a bounded revision and retest, narrow the workflow, pause it, or retire it. The scorecard is not a dashboard for its own sake.

[ON SCREEN: Usage signals with fictional aggregates]

Usage tells me whether the workflow is being reached and completed in its intended situation. In this synthetic example, I record eight eligible fictional requests, six drafts that reached human review, and two manual fallbacks. That does not mean the workflow has created value, and it does not tell me whether any individual operator performed well. A fallback can be a sensible choice because the case did not fit or a required source was unavailable.

[ON SCREEN: Highlight baseline and comparison limitation]

I compare this period with a baseline only when the conditions are comparable. Here the limitation is visible: the pilot included only routine fictional requests, so I cannot use it to make a claim about urgent or unusual requests. I also note source, staffing, scope, or form changes that could explain a difference. I do not hide those conditions to make a trend look cleaner.

[ON SCREEN: Quality and boundary checks]

Quality is separate from usage. I choose checks a qualified person can inspect. Did the brief contain the required fields? Were claims supported by the approved fictional source? Did it label missing information instead of filling a blank? Did the draft help the reviewer take the next action? Did it respect privacy, authorization, and scope boundaries?

[ON SCREEN: One synthetic check marked “Revise”]

In this fictional review, the output is marked Revise because the approved source link is difficult for an operator to find. That is valuable evidence. I do not let an AI-generated score decide quality, and I do not call the overall system successful because other rows look positive. I ask what workflow step failed, who was affected, and what safe correction is proportionate.

[ON SCREEN: Burden and adoption signals]

I also measure burden. A draft could reduce typing but add more source searching, reviewer corrections, or support demand. I can use a short friction question, reviewer feedback, and manual-fallback reasons to see that. I keep the collection proportionate and aggregate where practical. Measurement should help care for the process, not become covert monitoring or a basis for a consequential decision about a person.

[ON SCREEN: Open `Resources/Adoption Feedback Plan.md`]

Numbers do not replace feedback close to the work. This feedback plan gives each observation a route. After first use, I might ask: Did this case fit the stated condition? Could you complete the next step after review? What was missing, unclear, unsupported, or difficult to inspect? If a fallback was used, what prompted it?

[ON SCREEN: Highlight privacy boundary and authorized sensitive route]

A general feedback channel needs a data boundary. I ask for a safe case reference or sanitized description, not pasted records or credentials. A privacy, safety, authorization, or consequential-boundary concern goes to the organization’s authorized route, not into a survey or a community discussion. I do not simulate or investigate a real incident in this course example.

[ON SCREEN: Synthetic feedback pattern]

Here is a fictional pattern. Operators say the current source link is hard to find. Reviewers say the new missing-room-detail label is helping them see what needs confirmation. I record those as distinct observations. The decision is not to expand the system. It is to clarify onboarding and the SOP, then test whether the source cue is easier to use. I tell users what changed and what did not change, rather than collecting feedback that disappears into a form.

[ON SCREEN: Open `Resources/Change and Maintenance Log.md`]

Every change needs a visible trail. In this fictional material-change entry, I move the source-link cue into the pre-run check. The log states what changed, why, the safe evidence reference, affected workflow step, required approval, test, rollback or manual fallback, communication, owner, and current status.

[ON SCREEN: Highlight three-case fictional retest and rollback]

I do not make five edits at once and assume the latest draft is better. The Events Lead approves this bounded change, I test it on three fictional routine cases, and I keep the Manual Event Brief available if the test reveals a problem. The log points to approved locations; it does not duplicate confidential inputs or restricted incident details.

[ON SCREEN: Synthetic review agenda]

Then I set a review cadence that fits the workflow. I begin with stop-level concerns and unresolved escalations. I look at the safe scorecard summary, feedback patterns, manual fallbacks, source or dependency context, and open changes. I finish with a recorded decision, named owner, due point, and user communication. A meeting without a decision record can become an interesting conversation that changes nothing.

[ON SCREEN: Review decision options: maintain / clarify / retest / narrow / pause / retire]

A repeated critical boundary failure can require a pause before the next scheduled review. Several fallbacks for the same reason may mean the fit condition or onboarding is wrong. A small scorecard does not replace judgment; it gives the responsible people evidence they can interpret in context.

[ON SCREEN: Closing sequence: Signals → Feedback → Review → Change log]

Your work this week is to make learning operational. Measure a few things that connect to the user’s next action. Ask for feedback that can lead to a decision. Review patterns with the right people. Record the change, test it safely, and keep the fallback available. That is continuous improvement without pretending that activity or a single metric gives us certainty.

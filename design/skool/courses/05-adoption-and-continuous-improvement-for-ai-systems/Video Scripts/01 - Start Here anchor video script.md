# Start Here: Name the system, owner, and evidence before adoption

**Embed on:** `Start Here/01 - Confirm the system and operating owner.md`

**Estimated recording time:** 8–9 minutes

**Outcome:** Members will define a bounded operating system, name accountable roles and user conditions, and create a safe baseline reference before asking anyone to adopt the workflow.

## What to demonstrate on screen

Use only **Harborlight Community Center event-request brief workflow — SYNTHETIC TRAINING EXAMPLE**. It is fictional training material: a fictional front-desk operator prepares a draft event-request brief from an approved fictional form for an events lead to review. The system does **not** reserve rooms, approve access, set fees, make safety or accessibility decisions, or send communications. Do not open, search, paste, or display real member, client, employee, volunteer, event, or confidential data.

1. Open `Resources/System Ownership and Onboarding Template.md`. Show the empty **System definition** fields, then enter a synthetic one-sentence definition, scope, exclusions, approved system home, privacy boundary, and manual fallback. Keep every visible label marked `SYNTHETIC TRAINING EXAMPLE`.
2. In the template’s **Ownership record**, enter fictional roles: Events Operations Coordinator as daily operating owner; Venue Policy Lead as source owner; Events Lead as output reviewer; Training Systems Maintainer as technical-care contact; and Privacy and Access Lead as the escalation route. Show routine authority separately from approval authority.
3. Show the **User and adoption map** in `Resources/System Ownership and Onboarding Template.md`. Add synthetic rows for the front-desk operator, events-lead reviewer, facilities recipient, and affected event attendee. Point out the actual work moment, approved-input access, time pressure, accessibility needs, review requirement, and manual fallback for each role.
4. Show a plain local working note titled `Harborlight operating baseline — SYNTHETIC TRAINING EXAMPLE`. Record a fictional before-state for a small safe sample: `6 representative synthetic requests`, `4 needed a missing-detail follow-up`, `manual brief used when room details were unclear`. Label it an observation example, not a performance result or a promise.
5. Open `Resources/Usage and Quality Scorecard.md`. Point to **Baseline period or safe baseline reference**, **Known comparison limitations**, and the separation of usage, quality, burden, and boundary checks. Do not enter real measurements or individual user data.
6. Show a synthetic stop response for an incomplete or access-limited request: `Do not run the workflow. Use the fictional Manual Event Brief and route the approved reference to the Events Lead.`

## Teleprompter script

[ON SCREEN: Title card: “Adoption & Continuous Improvement for AI Systems — Start Here”]

Hi, I’m George. Before I ask people to adopt an AI-assisted workflow, I make sure we can name the system, the people responsible for it, and the evidence we will use to judge whether it fits the work.

[ON SCREEN: Simple flow: approved input → draft brief → human review → next action]

For this course, I am using the Harborlight Community Center event-request brief workflow. Harborlight is fictional, and every field on screen is synthetic training material. I am not showing real member, client, employee, volunteer, event, or confidential data. Use the same boundary in your own course work: practice with authorized, minimized, or clearly synthetic material.

[ON SCREEN: Synthetic system definition in `Resources/System Ownership and Onboarding Template.md`]

My bounded definition is: “For a fictional front-desk operator, this process uses an approved fictional event-request form to prepare a structured draft brief so an Events Lead can review the request and choose the next action.” That sentence tells me who uses it, what starts it, what it produces, and where human judgment remains.

[ON SCREEN: Highlight “What the system does not decide, authorize, or send on its own”]

The exclusions matter just as much. This system does not reserve a room, approve building access, set a fee, decide an accessibility accommodation, make a safety judgment, or send a final message. A draft can help someone prepare work. It does not inherit the authority of the person who reviews it.

[ON SCREEN: Ownership record with role rows]

Next, I name ownership. I do not assume that the person who created a prompt owns every operating decision. In this fictional example, the Events Operations Coordinator owns daily operation: they keep the current instructions available, receive routine questions, and coordinate small approved corrections. The Venue Policy Lead owns the current policy source. The Events Lead reviews the draft before a next action. The Training Systems Maintainer handles a tool or access problem within their authority. The Privacy and Access Lead is the route for a boundary concern.

[ON SCREEN: Highlight “Routine authority” and “Consult or escalate when”]

Writing these distinctions down prevents a common handoff problem. The operating owner can clarify a visible instruction or log an issue. They cannot silently reinterpret venue policy, change a data boundary, or approve a consequential decision. If one person holds several roles in a small team, I still make the separate responsibilities visible. I also name a backup and a shared approved location, so the system is not trapped in one person’s memory or inbox.

[ON SCREEN: User and adoption map]

Ownership is only part of adoption. I need to understand the people who touch the workflow. The front-desk operator may be working during a busy intake moment. They need access to the approved request form, a clear fit condition, enough time to inspect missing fields, and permission to use a manual fallback without blame. The Events Lead needs to see what is supported, what is missing, and what remains their decision. Facilities may need a usable final brief, not a copy of a private intake record. An affected attendee may need an accessible outcome, even though they never touch the system.

[ON SCREEN: Conditions list: access, permission, time, source, review, fallback]

Those are user conditions, not objections to work around. If a user lacks approved access, cannot find the current form, or needs an accommodation the common path does not support, the correct response may be to pause this path and use the existing manual route. Adoption is not forced use. It is a process that fits a real work moment and makes it safe to question or decline an uncertain draft.

[ON SCREEN: Local note: “Harborlight operating baseline — SYNTHETIC TRAINING EXAMPLE”]

Before launch, I capture a small baseline. Here I am using six fictional representative requests. This is only a demonstration of the record: four needed a follow-up for missing detail, and the manual brief was used when the room information was unclear. I am not claiming that these figures predict an outcome. A baseline is a truthful before-state, so later I can compare use, quality, burden, and fallback patterns with the right context.

[ON SCREEN: Open `Resources/Usage and Quality Scorecard.md`; highlight baseline and limitations]

I use the scorecard to record the baseline reference and any limits on comparison. Maybe the later cases are more complex, the source form changes, staffing changes, or the pilot scope narrows. If conditions changed, I say so. I do not turn a small sample into a verdict about people or use it for covert monitoring or a consequential decision about a worker.

[ON SCREEN: Synthetic incomplete-request card: “Room detail unavailable”]

Here is the stop route. If the room detail is missing or the operator does not have approved access to verify it, the workflow does not guess. The operator uses the fictional Manual Event Brief, records only a safe reference, and routes the question to the Events Lead. Keeping a manual fallback available is part of responsible adoption.

[ON SCREEN: Four starting records: system definition, ownership, user conditions, baseline]

Your first task is small but important. Define one active system in plain language. Name the operating owner and the human-review boundary. Map the conditions each primary user needs. Then capture a safe baseline of the current path. Do not promise improvement before you have operating evidence. Start with a process another person can inspect, question, and safely operate.

## Member action

1. Complete `Start Here/01 - Confirm the system and operating owner.md` and write one sentence that names the user, trigger, approved input, draft output, next action, and exclusions.
2. Copy `Resources/System Ownership and Onboarding Template.md` into an approved shared location. Name the operating owner, source owner, reviewer, privacy or access route, technical-care contact, backups, and manual fallback.
3. Complete the user and adoption map for the operator, reviewer, recipient, and affected person. Record access, time, information, accessibility, review, and fallback conditions.
4. Create a safe baseline record and connect it to `Resources/Usage and Quality Scorecard.md`. Use approved aggregate references, sanitized notes, or synthetic cases; do not use real member or client data in course work or community discussion.

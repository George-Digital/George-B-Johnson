# Week 3: Maintain responsibly, pause when needed, and make the 90-day decision

**Embed on:** `Week 3 — Maintain or Retire/01 - Monitor source and dependency changes.md`

**Estimated recording time:** 9–10 minutes

**Outcome:** Members will create a dependency-monitoring and maintenance routine, define pause and retirement actions with named authority and fallback, and prepare a 30-, 60-, and 90-day evidence-based operating decision.

## Teleprompter script

[ON SCREEN: Title card: “Week 3 — Maintain or Retire”]

Hi, I’m George. An AI-assisted workflow is not maintained because the prompt still opens. It depends on current sources, forms, permissions, tools, review capacity, and people who can make the right decisions. This week, I make those dependencies visible, decide what happens when one changes, and plan an honest 30-, 60-, and 90-day operating decision.

[ON SCREEN: Synthetic label: “Harborlight Community Center event-request brief workflow — SYNTHETIC TRAINING EXAMPLE”]

I am continuing with the fictional Harborlight workflow. It prepares a draft brief from a fictional approved event-request form for an Events Lead to review. It never reserves a room, grants access, sets a fee, decides safety or accessibility needs, or sends a message. Everything on screen is synthetic. I am not using real member, client, employee, volunteer, policy, access, or confidential information.

[ON SCREEN: Open `Resources/Change and Maintenance Log.md` at “Maintenance register”]

I begin with a dependency inventory. The visible AI step is only one part of the operating system. Here I list a fictional event-request form, venue-use policy, approved training workspace, drafting-tool configuration, and the Events Lead’s review role. I also include the current instructions, support and fallback patterns, ownership, and backup coverage.

[ON SCREEN: Highlight owner, trigger, evidence location, and next action]

For every dependency, I name an owner and a signal that could tell the operating owner something changed. The form owner may announce a new field. The policy owner may issue a revision. A technical contact may receive a tool or connector notice. An operator may report a new missing field or access denial. The operating owner should not be expected to discover all of this by chance.

[ON SCREEN: Synthetic maintenance row: Event Request Form v1.2]

The record also says which workflow step is affected and what action applies. A minor wording change might be noted and checked at the next review. A changed permission, a broken connection, a revised rule, unknown tool behavior, or a source conflict can require a retest or a pause. A notification alone does not tell me that the workflow still behaves appropriately. A qualified person needs to inspect the impact.

[ON SCREEN: Synthetic form change: v1.2 → v1.3; “new room-access field”]

Here is a fictional form change. Version 1.3 adds a required room-access field. The draft’s room-access section is now affected. I do not guess how the new field should be interpreted, and I do not allow an old instruction to hide the gap. I pause that section, keep the fictional Manual Event Brief available, and route the change to the Form Owner and Events Lead.

[ON SCREEN: Change-log entry: material; evidence reference; approval; safe retest]

The change log records the change category, safe evidence reference, affected users or workflow step, approval, test, rollback or fallback, communication, owner, and status. Once the form owner and reviewer confirm the intended interpretation, I run a representative synthetic test. The retest gives us evidence about this version and test case. It does not guarantee future reliability, and it does not authorize a new decision outside the workflow’s scope.

[ON SCREEN: Highlight maintenance duties and backup coverage]

Maintenance is recurring work, not an afterthought. Someone confirms that the current instructions are available. Someone watches sources and input forms. Someone reviews quality patterns. Someone handles technical care. Someone tells users when the process changes or stops. I give each task one accountable owner, a boundary on their authority, the access they need, a backup, and a calendar or event trigger.

[ON SCREEN: “No review capacity” maintenance row → “Narrow or pause”]

Capacity matters. If the qualified Events Lead cannot review the output, continued use may not be responsible even if the tool is technically available. The decision can be to narrow the workflow or pause it, rather than asking users to work around an absent review gate. Maintenance includes the authority to say no to an expansion that the current owners cannot support.

[ON SCREEN: Open `Resources/Retirement and 90 Day Review Template.md` at “Pause and retirement record”]

Now I define pause and retirement criteria before they become inconvenient. A pause is a temporary stop for all or part of the workflow while we investigate, correct, or obtain approval. Retirement is a deliberate decision to end or replace the process when it no longer has a supported purpose, owner, permission, evidence base, or maintenance capacity.

[ON SCREEN: Pause triggers: source/dependency, critical quality, privacy/access, missing review, poor fit]

My pause triggers are observable. A source or dependency changes in a material way. A critical quality check fails. A privacy, access, authorization, or consequential-boundary concern appears. Review capacity disappears. Or manual fallbacks show that the system does not fit the work. I do not use “people do not like it” as a vague trigger, but I do take repeated user confusion or pressure to accept drafts seriously as operating evidence.

[ON SCREEN: Synthetic policy change pause card]

Here is a fictional pause decision. A venue-policy revision changes room-access rules, and I cannot yet verify what that means for the current instructions. I pause the affected room-access section, tell operators to use the Manual Event Brief, and route the question to the Venue Policy Lead. The workflow does not resume because a deadline is uncomfortable. It resumes only after the named safe test and authorized approval occur.

[ON SCREEN: Highlight pause authority, investigation owner, resumption authority, communication]

The record names who can pause, who investigates, who can authorize resumption, who communicates with users, and what happens to access and records if the process is retired. A narrow pause can protect a useful part of the workflow while an affected part is reviewed. Users should never discover a pause because an old link silently stops working.

[ON SCREEN: 30-day stabilization review]

The 30-day review asks whether the common path is stable. Can users find current onboarding and the SOP? Are review, support, fallback, and escalation routes working? Did a source, permission, or dependency change affect use? I make only clear, bounded corrections supported by evidence, and I log them.

[ON SCREEN: 60-day adoption and maintenance review]

At 60 days, I compare the scorecard with the baseline carefully. I look at intended use, quality reviews, manual fallbacks, user and reviewer burden, source care, support patterns, and maintenance capacity. I state any changes in case mix, staffing, scope, or source quality that limit comparison. I confirm that named owners and backups are actually doing the work, not merely appearing in a table.

[ON SCREEN: 90-day operating decision]

At 90 days, I bring the safe evidence together: usage and quality trends with limitations, feedback themes, source and dependency status, privacy and access boundaries, review capacity, open risks, and the current manual fallback. The decision options remain explicit: maintain the current scope, improve and retest, narrow scope, pause, replace, or retire.

[ON SCREEN: Decision record: reason, authority, user communication, next review or closure]

I record the reason, decision authority, user communication, next review date, or closeout action. If retirement is the responsible choice, I point users to the replacement or manual process, mark current instructions retired, change access as authorized, and retain or delete records according to approved requirements. Retirement is not a failure. Continuing a system without ownership, evidence, permissions, or review capacity is the greater risk.

[ON SCREEN: Closing sequence: monitor → maintain → pause/retest → 30/60/90 decision]

Your job this week is to make continued use a decision, not an assumption. Monitor the sources and dependencies that shape the workflow. Name the people and triggers that maintain it. Keep pause and fallback routes ready. Then use the 30-, 60-, and 90-day plan to choose the next responsible scope—or to end the process cleanly when that is the evidence-based decision.

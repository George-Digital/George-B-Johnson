# Week 1: Launch with onboarding, an SOP, support, and a bounded pilot

**Embed on:** `Week 1 — Launch and Handoff/01 - Create user onboarding.md`

**Estimated recording time:** 9–10 minutes

**Outcome:** Members will prepare a first-use onboarding guide, a reviewable operating SOP and support route, and a bounded pilot plan that keeps human review, privacy boundaries, and the manual fallback visible.

## What to demonstrate on screen

Use only **Harborlight Community Center event-request brief workflow — SYNTHETIC TRAINING EXAMPLE**. The fictional workflow prepares a draft brief from a fictional approved request form for an Events Lead to review; it never reserves rooms, approves access, sets fees, decides safety or accessibility needs, or sends messages. Do not display real member, client, employee, volunteer, event, or confidential information.

1. Open `Resources/System Ownership and Onboarding Template.md` to **Answer-first onboarding guide**. Enter a synthetic opening orientation, one fit condition, one exclusion, the approved fictional input location, the review point, support route, and manual fallback.
2. Demonstrate a safe common-path synthetic request: `Neighborhood craft workshop — fictional`. Show only made-up fields. Walk through locating the current instructions, checking that the case fits, labeling a missing detail rather than inventing it, producing a draft, and handing it to the fictional Events Lead.
3. Demonstrate a stop example: a fictional request includes an unverified accessibility need or an unavailable room detail. Show `Do not infer. Use the Manual Event Brief and route to the Events Lead or the authorized accessibility process.`
4. Open `Resources/Operating SOP and Support Template.md`. Show the **Operating SOP** table, **Review gate**, **Support plan**, and **Safe issue record**. Populate only synthetic fields for trigger, approved source, AI-assisted action, human decision, safe reference, and exception response.
5. In the support table, demonstrate the `Expected review window or event` field for every shown row. Distinguish a routine how-to question from a source conflict and a privacy, safety, authorization, or consequential-boundary concern: enter an operating-owner-approved review cadence for the routine question, an owner-approved source-review event for the conflict, and an authorized stop-and-route event for the concern. Show the fictional daily support thread for ordinary questions and an authorized organizational route for stop-level concerns. Explain that this field states an owner-approved cadence or event trigger, not a response-time promise; do not simulate an incident.
6. Show a simple synthetic bounded-pilot card: `3 fictional front-desk operators`, `5 fictional routine requests or 1 week`, included cases, excluded cases, midpoint check, usage and quality observations, stop triggers, reviewer, and manual fallback. Use `Resources/Usage and Quality Scorecard.md` only to point to the safe observation fields.

## Teleprompter script

[ON SCREEN: Title card: “Week 1 — Launch and Handoff”]

Hi, I’m George. A launch is not the moment when I hand someone a clever prompt and hope they remember what to do. It is the moment when I make the common path, the human-review point, the support route, and the stop route usable in real work.

[ON SCREEN: Synthetic label: “Harborlight Community Center event-request brief workflow — SYNTHETIC TRAINING EXAMPLE”]

I am continuing with our fictional Harborlight event-request brief workflow. Everything on screen is invented for training. The workflow turns a fictional approved request form into a draft brief for an Events Lead. It does not reserve anything, authorize access, make safety or accessibility decisions, or send a final communication. I will not use real member, client, staff, volunteer, or event data.

[ON SCREEN: Open `Resources/System Ownership and Onboarding Template.md` at “Answer-first onboarding guide”]

I start onboarding with the answer a new operator needs at the work moment. Mine says: “Use this workflow for a routine fictional event request with an approved form; it prepares a draft brief for Events Lead review and does not confirm a room, fee, access, or accommodation.” This is more useful than a long tour. It tells the operator what the system is for, when it fits, and what remains outside its authority.

[ON SCREEN: Highlight “Use it when,” “Do not use it when,” and “Before you start, confirm”]

Before a first run, I ask the operator to check the scope, the approved source, required fields, data boundary, and current instruction version. I show where those instructions live at the point of use. A user should not have to search an old chat thread to find the approved current process.

[ON SCREEN: Synthetic common-path request: “Neighborhood craft workshop — fictional”]

Here is one safe common-path example. The operator opens a fictional approved request, confirms it is routine and in scope, and checks the date, expected attendance, and setup request. The AI-assisted step creates a structured draft. The operator then checks that missing information is visibly labeled and sends the draft to the fictional Events Lead for review. The Events Lead—not the draft—chooses the next authorized action.

[ON SCREEN: Missing field visibly labeled: “Room detail: confirmation required”]

I deliberately show an incomplete case. The room detail is not available. The right behavior is not to pull a value from memory, a past request, or an unapproved location. I label it as needing confirmation. If the missing detail prevents a safe handoff, I stop the workflow and use the manual path.

[ON SCREEN: Synthetic stop card: “Accessibility need unverified — do not infer; route to authorized process”]

Here is a second stop example. A fictional request contains an unverified accessibility need. This workflow does not decide what accommodation is appropriate. The operator uses the Manual Event Brief and routes the approved reference to the Events Lead or the authorized accessibility process. A pause or escalation is a correct operating outcome, not evidence that the user failed onboarding.

[ON SCREEN: Open `Resources/Operating SOP and Support Template.md`]

Once I can demonstrate the common path, I write it into an SOP. This template makes each step observable. I record the trigger, approved input location, authoritative source, AI-assisted action, human check, safe record, and response when the case is incomplete, conflicting, or out of scope.

[ON SCREEN: SOP table; highlight AI-assisted action versus human check]

I keep the assistance and the human decision separate. An operator may run the draft step. A reviewer checks required elements against approved sources. An authorized person decides whether to act. The SOP also says what data is permitted, what stays in an approved system, and where the manual fallback lives. I never copy sensitive inputs into a casual log just to show that a step occurred.

[ON SCREEN: Review gate]

The review gate tells a new reviewer what to inspect. In this fictional workflow, they verify that required fields are present, missing details are visible, claims are supported by the approved request, and the draft has not crossed its scope. “Ready to use,” “revise,” and “stop and escalate” are distinct outcomes. A polished paragraph is not enough to pass a review gate.

[ON SCREEN: Support plan rows: routine / source conflict / stop-level concern; highlight `Expected review window or event`]

Support needs the same clarity. For each row, I complete `Expected review window or event` as an operating-owner-approved review cadence or event trigger—not a response-time promise. For the routine question about finding current instructions, the fictional operating owner has approved review at the scheduled daily support review. For a source conflict or repeated quality issue, I enter the owner-approved source-review checkpoint or the event that the approved source version changes. For a privacy, safety, authorization, or consequential-boundary concern, I enter the event: concern identified; stop the affected path and route it through the organization’s authorized channel. The user does not wait in the ordinary queue for that stop action. I make the route, the review cadence or trigger, and the manual fallback clear without promising constant availability or a response time.

[ON SCREEN: Safe issue record fields]

A useful issue record captures what the operator expected, what happened, the impact, the version, the safe reference, and the fallback or stop action. It does not contain credentials, raw sensitive records, or restricted details. Repeated questions become evidence for a clearer guide or a process change.

[ON SCREEN: Synthetic bounded-pilot card]

Now I am ready for a bounded pilot, not a broad release. This fictional pilot has three front-desk operators, up to five routine requests or one week, a named reviewer, and a midpoint check. It excludes urgent requests, access decisions, fee disputes, private records, and any safety or accessibility determination. The manual brief remains available for every participant.

[ON SCREEN: Pilot preflight: onboarding, SOP, support, source access, privacy, fallback]

Before the first case, I confirm that onboarding, the SOP, source access, support, review, and fallback are genuinely available. During the pilot, I observe a small safe set of signals: whether users can complete the common path, whether the reviewer finds required elements, what caused a fallback, and what friction appears. A signal invites investigation; it does not prove the workflow is successful.

[ON SCREEN: Midpoint review and pause triggers]

At the midpoint, I review patterns. If users cannot access the approved form, a source changes, quality drops, or a stop-level concern appears, I pause or narrow the affected path. I do not continue merely to hit a case target. At the end, the decision can be to maintain the defined scope, revise and retest, narrow it, pause it, or retire it. That decision belongs to the people with the right authority and evidence.

[ON SCREEN: Closing sequence: Onboard → SOP → Support → Bounded pilot]

This week, make launch operational. Give a first-time user an answer-first guide. Make the SOP and review gate inspectable. Show where questions go and when the process stops. Then pilot a small, safe slice of real work conditions without making promises the evidence cannot support.

# Create the minimum useful SOP

**A minimum useful SOP is the shortest reliable common-path sequence that tells a capable person what starts the work, what to check, what to produce, and when to stop or hand off.**

Turn your decision rules into a simple operating procedure for the common case. Keep the SOP small enough to use during real work, while making the review, handoff, and exceptions visible.

A decision-rule list is valuable, but it can still leave a new operator wondering what happens first. An SOP, or standard operating procedure, gives the work an order. It says how the workflow begins, what materials are gathered, which checks happen before an output is created, who reviews it, and how the work ends. I think of it as the route through the common case, not a giant manual for every possibility.

The word “minimum” matters. I am not asking you to document every tool click, every historical exception, or every piece of background knowledge in your domain. That produces a document people avoid. I want the smallest procedure that lets a capable person run the workflow consistently enough to create and review the intended output.

I start by naming the trigger. What event should cause this procedure to begin? It might be a completed intake form, an incoming request, a scheduled review, or a new row in a shared sheet. A weak trigger is “when needed.” A useful trigger is “when a project lead submits a completed discovery-note form.” Clear starts prevent the system from being used on the wrong work or sitting idle because nobody knows who owns the first move.

Then I lay out the common path in verbs: gather, check, classify, draft, review, deliver, record. Each step should have one owner and a visible result. “Review the request” is too loose on its own. “Check that the request includes the service, deadline, and approval owner; list missing fields” is a step someone can perform and another person can inspect.

This is where the decision rules enter the procedure. Do not paste every rule into every step. Link the relevant rule to the point where it changes the route. For example, after gathering the input, the SOP might say: “Check required fields. If a required field is missing, use the missing-information rule and pause the draft.” That preserves a usable flow while keeping the judgment explicit.

Every minimum SOP needs a review point and a stop condition. The review point tells us who is accountable for the output before it reaches the user. The stop condition tells us what the system must not decide on its own. I include both even in low-risk workflows because they prevent a polished draft from being mistaken for a finished decision. If the input conflicts, lacks key evidence, or crosses a boundary you defined, the right next step may be to pause and ask a person.

Do not assume AI makes the procedure unnecessary. AI may summarize source material, assemble a draft, classify a predictable input, or flag missing fields. It does not own the workflow. I make the human responsibilities concrete: provide approved source material, verify factual claims, resolve conflicts, approve the final output, and update the procedure when evidence changes. If no person owns those decisions, the process will drift.

Write the SOP so it can survive a busy day. Use plain language, short steps, and the names of artifacts rather than abstract intentions. A colleague should be able to open it, see the input they need, follow the common route, and know exactly where to ask for help. Include a version date or a short revision note so people can tell which procedure is current.

Once you draft it, run the SOP yourself on one real or realistic safe input. Notice where you have to improvise. Every improvisation is a useful signal: perhaps a decision rule is missing, a step is in the wrong order, the input definition is too vague, or the common path is not actually common. Revise the document instead of relying on memory next time.

A short SOP is not a claim that the workflow has become automatic. It is a shared starting point. It makes the work easier to delegate, test, critique, and improve without erasing the operator’s responsibility for the outcome.

## Concrete example

A project coordinator prepares a kickoff brief after a client agrees to a new website project. The SOP starts when the signed scope and discovery notes are available. The coordinator gathers those documents, checks for missing launch dates and approval contacts, creates a kickoff brief from the approved template, and sends it to the project lead for review. If the scope and discovery notes conflict, the procedure stops and the project lead resolves the conflict before the brief goes out. AI may format a first draft, but the coordinator and lead verify the source details.

## Action checklist

- Name the specific event that starts the common workflow.
- List the minimum inputs required before work can proceed.
- Write the common path as five to seven action steps.
- Attach the relevant decision rule to each point where the path can change.
- Identify the output, reviewer, handoff, and stop condition.
- Run the SOP on one safe example and revise one unclear step.

## Required deliverable

Add a **Minimum Useful SOP** to your System Pack. Include the trigger, required inputs, five to seven ordered steps, linked decision points, output, human reviewer, handoff, stop or escalation condition, and one revision made after a test.

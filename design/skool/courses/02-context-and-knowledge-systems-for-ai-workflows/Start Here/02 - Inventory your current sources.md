# Inventory your current sources

**A source inventory makes hidden knowledge visible by recording what each source contains, who owns it, how authoritative it is, who may access it, and when it needs review.**

I inventory the sources that already shape the workflow before I choose a structure, because reliable context starts with knowing what exists and what should not be used.

Most teams have more context than they can use well. It is spread across policy documents, project notes, email threads, shared drives, product pages, spreadsheets, recorded decisions, and people’s memory. The problem is not always a shortage of material. It is that the current source, the approved source, the useful example, and an old draft often look equally available. An AI-assisted workflow will reproduce that confusion unless I make the distinctions visible.

I begin with a narrow collection window tied to the use case. If I am building context for an onboarding brief, I do not collect every document the company has ever created. I list the sources an operator actually consults for the common path: the current service scope, approved intake notes, current policy, output template, and known exception guidance. Then I add the sources that people reach for when something goes wrong. This gives me a practical starting map instead of an archive project.

For each source, I record a stable name or reference, source type, purpose, owner, last known update, and access boundary. I also ask a more important question: what kind of knowledge is inside it? A policy may contain rules. A signed scope may contain case-specific facts. A past handoff may be a useful example but not authority for a new case. A meeting note may record a decision, but I need to know whether that decision was approved, superseded, or informal.

I do not mark a source “trusted” because it sounds official. I ask who is accountable for it, what workflow it governs, and how I can tell whether it is current. If two documents disagree, an inventory does not resolve the disagreement by itself. It gives me a way to see it before the conflict becomes a misleading answer. I can then name the owner who decides which source controls.

Access is a field in every entry. I record who is allowed to view the material, who may add or change it, and whether the source contains information that should not be copied into a general context pack. I minimize what I collect. A workflow may need a service tier or approved contact role, but not an entire customer record. I do not move private, confidential, or sensitive information into a shared template merely to make retrieval easier. If access is uncertain, I pause and ask the designated owner.

I also capture a retrieval cue: the title, case type, date range, subject, decision identifier, or other label an operator could use to find the source again. A source that cannot be located or recognized is difficult to verify later. This is not a demand for a complex database. It is a practical way to make a claim traceable when a reviewer asks, “Where did that come from?”

The inventory needs a disposition. Each item is a candidate for the first pack, a reference that remains outside it, an example to label clearly, or a source to exclude. Exclusion is useful. An obsolete draft, unapproved note, or document with an unclear owner should not quietly become context because it happens to be nearby. I retain a note explaining why it is excluded so another person does not reintroduce it without review.

I treat people as sources carefully. An experienced operator can explain context that has never been documented, and that conversation may uncover an important rule. But a remembered practice is not automatically an approved rule. I capture the interview as a candidate, identify the owner who can validate it, and label it as pending until that review occurs. Human expertise is valuable; authority still needs to be explicit.

By the end of this exercise, I do not expect a perfect catalog. I expect a source map I can inspect with the owner: these are the materials for the common path, these are authoritative, these are examples, these need review, and these are out of bounds. That clarity is enough to begin structuring the context responsibly.

## Concrete example

For a customer-success renewal brief, I inventory the current account summary, approved service terms, renewal timeline, support-history summary, and prior briefing template. The account summary is case-specific and access-controlled; the service terms are authoritative for standard commitments; last quarter’s brief is an example only. An old sales slide is excluded because its owner cannot confirm that the features and pricing are current. The account manager reviews any customer-specific claim before use.

## Action checklist

- Copy the Context Inventory and Source Map Template.
- List the sources used on the common workflow path.
- Record purpose, owner, last-known update, authority status, and access boundary for each.
- Label each item as fact source, rule source, example, decision record, candidate, or exclude.
- Add a retrieval cue and a review date or unknown-status note.
- Flag sources with unclear ownership, permissions, or currency for human review.

## Required deliverable

Complete a first **Context Inventory and Source Map** with at least five sources or source categories, including their owner, authority status, access boundary, retrieval cue, and keep, review, or exclude disposition.

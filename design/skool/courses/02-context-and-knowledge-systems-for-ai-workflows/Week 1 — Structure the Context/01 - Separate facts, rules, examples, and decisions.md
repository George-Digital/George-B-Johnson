# Separate facts, rules, examples, and decisions

**Facts describe a specific case, rules govern repeatable handling, examples illustrate a pattern, and decisions remain accountable choices made by authorized people.**

I separate these four kinds of context before I build a pack, because mixing them is one of the fastest ways to make an AI-assisted workflow sound certain when it should ask, cite, or escalate.

A context pack becomes confusing when every document is treated as the same kind of truth. A project note can contain a fact about one client. A policy can contain a rule for a repeatable process. A good prior output can show an example of useful format. A manager’s judgment can record a decision for a particular exception. All four may be valuable, but they answer different questions and carry different authority.

I start with facts. Facts are statements tied to a particular case, time, source, or record: a stated deadline, a product version, a venue address, an account owner, or a request received on a specific date. I want facts to carry a source reference and, where it matters, a date or status. A fact that is not available in the approved material should be labeled unknown or missing. I do not let a strong example or a familiar pattern fill a factual gap.

Rules are different. A rule tells the operator or system how to respond when a condition appears. “If the approved intake lacks a deadline, request confirmation before preparing a final brief” is a rule. I attach it to an owning policy, procedure, or accountable person. Rules may expire or change, so I do not hide them inside a past example. I also distinguish a rule from a preference. “Use a concise opening” may be a style preference. “Do not release a safety instruction without the current approved procedure” is a boundary that needs explicit authority.

Examples teach shape and judgment without becoming case evidence. A sample handoff can show where missing information appears, how a source reference is written, and how a reviewer sees a stop condition. I label it as an example and remove or replace details that could be mistaken for facts in a live case. I tell the operator and any AI-assisted workflow not to borrow names, dates, quantities, commitments, or conclusions from an example. The current approved sources must support the current output.

Decisions are accountable choices. A decision may resolve a conflict between two sources, approve a temporary exception, choose a project path, or decide that a case needs escalation. I record who made it, when, what evidence they used, what scope it applies to, and when it should be reconsidered. A decision is not just another rule. It may be limited to one case, require a qualified role, or be superseded by a later decision. AI can summarize the evidence and draft options, but it does not own the decision.

This separation changes how I structure retrieval. If I need to know the current shipping date for an order, I retrieve the case fact. If I need to know what to do when a date is missing, I retrieve the operating rule. If I am showing a new coordinator the expected output, I retrieve an approved example. If two sources disagree, I retrieve the prior decision only to understand its scope, then route a new case to the current owner when appropriate. The label tells me what the material may and may not do.

I make the distinctions visible in the pack. A simple section label, source tag, or short note can do the job: Fact, Rule, Example, or Decision. I avoid giving a large mixed document to an operator and expecting them to infer the difference. Explicit labels support human review because the reviewer can ask, “Is this claim a current fact, a general rule, or an illustration?” They also support access boundaries: a decision record may have a narrower audience than a general rule, and a case fact may not belong in a shared learning example.

There will be gray areas. A policy may include facts about its effective date and rules about behavior. A meeting note may capture both a case fact and a decision. I do not force the whole document into one label. I point to the relevant passage, preserve its source, and mark its role in the workflow. If classification is uncertain, I ask the source owner rather than making an assumption permanent.

This work is not about making the pack academic. It is how I keep a system honest. Facts need evidence, rules need authority, examples need labels, and decisions need accountable people. Once those boundaries are clear, the context can be useful without pretending every piece of text carries the same weight.

## Concrete example

A project kickoff pack includes the signed scope as a fact source for deliverables, an approved onboarding procedure as the rule source for required checks, a sanitized prior kickoff brief as an example of format, and a project-lead note approving a one-time deadline exception as a decision. The coordinator may use the first three to prepare a draft, but the deadline exception is limited to its named project. The project lead reviews any new exception.

## Action checklist

- Review each candidate source or passage in my inventory.
- Label it as a fact, rule, example, decision, or a mixed source with separate passages.
- Add a source reference and date or status to case-specific facts.
- Name the authority or owner behind each rule.
- Label examples so they cannot be mistaken for live case evidence.
- Record who made each decision, its scope, and when human review is required.

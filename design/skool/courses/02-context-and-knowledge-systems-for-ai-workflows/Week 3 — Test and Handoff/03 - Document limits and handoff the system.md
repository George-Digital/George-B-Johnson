# Document limits and handoff the system

**A responsible handoff tells the next operator what the context system supports, which sources and versions are approved, who owns updates and review, what access applies, and when the workflow must stop.**

I hand off the first context system as an honest operating package, not as a claim that every future question has been solved or that human review is no longer needed.

A context pack is only useful beyond its builder if another capable person can open it and answer a few essential questions. What is this for? Who is allowed to use it? Which sources are current and authoritative? How do I prepare or retrieve context for the common path? How do I check a draft? What do I do when a source is missing, stale, conflicting, or restricted? Who owns the next update? A handoff should make those answers visible before a person uses the system under pressure.

I begin with the use case and scope. I restate the intended user, bounded task, approved output, and explicit exclusions. I do not describe the pack as a general expert or an automated decision-maker. If the workflow touches health, law, finances, employment, safety, eligibility, discipline, or access to essential services, I state clearly that AI assistance remains advisory and a qualified human makes the final decision. The handoff needs to preserve that authority boundary, not bury it in a footnote.

Next, I point to the current pack and approved source locations. I include the version date, pack owner, source owners, and the review or expiration status of each active source. I distinguish the active working view from archive material, candidates, examples, and superseded content. I do not copy restricted source contents into the handoff to make it feel complete. I name the approved access path and the role that can verify a claim when the next operator lacks permission.

I document the run and review sequence in simple language. The operator confirms they have the approved input, uses the current pack structure or intake process, prepares a bounded draft, traces important claims, applies the grounding checklist, and sends the result to the named reviewer. The reviewer checks source authority, currency, access appropriateness, missing or conflicting context, and any consequential judgment. Only the authorized human approves the outcome or decides that the system should pause.

Limits need their own section. I name source gaps, unsupported request types, known failure modes from tests, unresolved conflicts, temporary assumptions, access limitations, and items awaiting owner review. I also state what the system must not do: invent case facts, treat examples as current rules, use expired material, disclose restricted information, or make final decisions outside its authority. A limitation is not an embarrassment. It is a practical instruction that protects the user and guides the next improvement.

I make the escalation route specific. For a missing fact, the operator requests the approved source or owner confirmation. For a source conflict, they preserve the references and route it to the authority owner. For restricted material, they use the approved handoff to an authorized reviewer. For an out-of-scope or high-impact request, they stop the AI-assisted path and route it to the qualified decision-maker. The pack does not need to solve every exception; it needs to prevent a person from improvising an unsafe answer.

I include the maintenance handoff. The next owner should know how to submit a new source, what status it has before approval, which events trigger an update, how to record a change, what needs retesting, and when the next review occurs. I identify a backup contact or role where appropriate. If ownership is not accepted or access cannot be confirmed, I do not call the handoff complete. I restrict the system to review-only use or pause it until the responsible organization establishes the boundary.

I ask the receiving operator or reviewer to perform a short walkthrough with a safe case. They should locate the controlling source, distinguish a fact from an example, trace an important claim, explain the missing-context route, and identify who approves the final result. Their questions are handoff evidence. I revise the overview, labels, or runbook if they cannot find an answer without guessing.

Finally, I record the handoff date, recipients or roles, version, orientation result, accepted limitations, open issues, and next review date. I do not ask a recipient to accept responsibility for content or decisions they are not authorized to own. The handoff is complete when responsibilities are clear and the system is usable for its limited scope with human review—not when a folder has merely been shared.

## Concrete example

A volunteer-program coordinator hands off an event-brief context pack to a new operations lead. The handoff states that it supports draft shift briefs from the current approved schedule, role list, and venue instructions. It links to the access-controlled sources, labels a prior brief as example only, and names the event lead as reviewer for safety and schedule conflicts. The new lead runs a synthetic missing-access-instructions case, finds the escalation route, and records a question about expiration of temporary venue guidance. The pack owner updates the handoff note and schedules the next review.

## Action checklist

- Copy the Context System Handoff Template.
- State the use case, scope, exclusions, current version, and human-review boundary.
- List active sources, owners, status, approved locations, and access roles.
- Document the common-path run, grounding review, missing-context route, conflict route, and stop conditions.
- Name known limitations, unresolved questions, and prohibited uses.
- Record update process, backup ownership, required retests, handoff recipients, and next review date.
- Run a safe receiving-operator walkthrough and revise any unclear instruction.

## Required deliverable

Complete and save a **Context System Handoff** containing the current Context Pack reference, scope, source and access map, ownership, run and review sequence, limitations, escalation routes, maintenance plan, safe walkthrough evidence, and next review date.

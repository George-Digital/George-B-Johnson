# Set ownership, updates, and expiration rules

**Trustworthy context has named owners, clear update triggers, visible review dates, and expiration rules that remove or route material before it is mistaken for current guidance.**

I give each active source and pack section an owner and a maintenance rule, because a useful document becomes risky when nobody knows who can confirm, update, or retire it.

Knowledge does not become reliable because it was reviewed once. Policies change, project facts age, people leave roles, product details move, and an exception that made sense last month becomes misleading in the next case. The maintenance question is not whether everything can be kept current forever. It is whether an operator can see what is current enough for the task, what is pending review, and who must decide when that status is unclear.

I start by naming ownership at more than one level. A source owner is accountable for the accuracy and approval status of a particular policy, record, or reference. A pack owner is accountable for keeping the working structure, links, labels, and change log usable. An operational reviewer is accountable for checking the output in the real workflow. One person may hold several roles in a small team, but I write the roles separately so a handoff does not depend on memory.

Then I define update triggers. Some sources should be reviewed on a calendar cadence: monthly, quarterly, annually, or at another interval that suits their volatility and impact. Others should be reviewed when an event occurs: a policy revision, product release, ownership change, new exception, source conflict, failed grounding test, or repeated user question. I avoid a generic “keep this updated.” A trigger tells the owner when work is due and tells an operator why a familiar source might need rechecking.

Expiration is different from review. A review date says, “Check whether this remains appropriate.” An expiration date says, “Do not use this after this point unless an owner renews it.” Case facts, temporary decisions, and time-limited guidance often need expiration. I label expired material visibly and remove it from the active retrieval path. I retain it only as the approved recordkeeping practice requires, with an explanation that it is not current context.

I choose a practical status vocabulary: current, pending review, superseded, expired, and retired. These labels must change how the workflow behaves. A current approved rule may be used. A pending source may be shown as a candidate but not used to support an important claim. A superseded or expired source should not drive a new output. When status is absent or uncertain, the operator pauses and asks the owner rather than assuming the item is current.

The update path must preserve traceability. When I revise a rule or pack section, I record what changed, why, who approved it, the effective date, the prior version or source reference, and what needs retesting. I do not need elaborate software to start; a concise change log is enough. What matters is that another reviewer can see why the active version changed and whether the test evidence still applies.

I plan for absences too. If the named owner is unavailable, the pack should list the backup role or escalation route. I do not allow a stale source to become permanent just because the usual owner is on leave. If no authorized owner can review a consequential change, I restrict or pause the affected workflow. Human review is a required control, not an administrative detail to bypass when the process is inconvenient.

Access decisions may change with ownership. When a role changes, I review who can view, edit, approve, or share the pack and its sources. I do not assume that a former contributor retains access or that a new operator should see all historical material. The approved environment and responsible access process determine permissions; the pack records the boundary and the person who can approve changes.

I test the maintenance rule with a simple scenario. What happens when a policy becomes effective next week? What happens when an example is six months old? What happens when a source owner leaves? If the answer is only “someone will notice,” I improve the trigger, owner, or status. The system should make the safe next action clear before a stale item reaches a draft.

A small set of maintained sources beats a large collection that nobody owns. Ownership, updates, and expiration rules make the first context pack something a team can responsibly keep using and improve.

## Concrete example

A customer-support pack lists the support-policy lead as owner of operating rules, the support operations coordinator as pack owner, and the shift lead as output reviewer. The returns policy is reviewed quarterly and immediately after any approved policy change. A temporary holiday exception expires on its stated end date and is removed from active retrieval. If the policy lead is unavailable, the operations manager is the backup approver. Until approval, a changed rule remains pending and the draft routes uncertain cases to the shift lead.

## Action checklist

- Name the source owner, pack owner, operational reviewer, and backup escalation role.
- Set a review cadence or event trigger for each active source category.
- Add expiration rules for temporary facts, decisions, and time-limited guidance.
- Use visible current, pending, superseded, expired, and retired status labels.
- Record change reason, approval, effective date, prior reference, and retest need.
- Review access permissions when ownership or role responsibilities change.

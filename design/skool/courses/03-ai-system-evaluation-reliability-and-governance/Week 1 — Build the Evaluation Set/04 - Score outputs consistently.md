# Score outputs consistently

**I score outputs against written evidence and a shared rubric so a pass, revision, or stop decision does not depend on who happened to review the draft.**

I make scoring repeatable by defining the unit of review, recording evidence for each criterion, and calibrating reviewers on the same safe cases.

A score is only useful when it represents an observable judgment. I do not assign a number because numbers look rigorous. I use a scoring method to help a reviewer answer: did this output meet the acceptance criteria for its intended use, what evidence supports that conclusion, and what happens next? If I cannot show the source, output section, or behavior behind a score, I have an opinion rather than a review record.

I start by defining the unit I will score. It may be one generated brief, one routing decision, one complete run, or one required output field. I keep the unit aligned with the intended use. If a user receives a whole handoff brief, I score the brief and its gates, not isolated sentences that look good on their own. If the workflow has a critical escalation step, I record whether the route occurred; elegant prose does not make up for a missed stop condition.

I choose a simple scale. For many early systems, pass, revise, and fail gate are enough. A three-point scale can help when I need to distinguish fully met, partly met, and not met. The important part is defining each level. “Completeness: 3” means all required fields are present or correctly labeled unknown; “2” means a noncritical item needs revision; “1” means a required element is missing or the case cannot move forward. I do not use an average to hide a failed privacy, source, or high-impact boundary gate.

I score criterion by criterion and write a short evidence note. I compare material claims to the approved source, check the required fields, inspect the handling of missing information, verify the route, and confirm that private or restricted information stayed within the allowed boundary. I save a secure output reference or a sanitized excerpt rather than copying confidential records into a general rubric. The notes should let a second reviewer understand why I marked a result without needing to rely on memory.

Consistency requires calibration. I ask two appropriate reviewers to score the same small set of safe outputs independently when the stakes or volume justify it. Then I compare the scores and discuss the evidence behind any disagreement. If one reviewer thinks “clear next action” means a recommended owner and another expects a deadline, I refine the criterion. I do not force agreement by averaging away the difference. The disagreement tells me where the rubric, source rule, or ownership boundary needs clarification.

I also watch for reviewer drift. Over time, a team may become more lenient because they are familiar with the output, or more severe because one recent failure is vivid. I use periodic calibration cases and a documented rubric version to make changes visible. If I change a scoring rule, I record the date, reason, and which previous cases may no longer be comparable.

Scores support decisions; they do not replace them. I set an overall rule in advance, such as: all gates must pass and four of five scored criteria must pass for the draft to enter human approval. A result below that threshold triggers a defined path: revise, request missing information, use a fallback, or escalate. In work that affects people’s health, legal rights, employment, finances, housing, benefits, education access, safety, discipline, or essential services, a qualified human still examines the evidence and makes the final decision. The score describes output quality, not a person’s eligibility or worth.

I look for patterns after scoring several cases. A repeated omission may point to an input problem. A pattern of reviewer disagreement may reveal a vague acceptance criterion. A single severe boundary failure may warrant a pause even if most ordinary cases score well. I record the pattern in the failure or change log and make one bounded revision at a time so the next result teaches me something.

## Concrete example

I score a set of service-request briefs using five criteria: source grounding, required fields, missing-information handling, correct routing, and usability for the coordinator. A brief that includes every field but invents an access code fails the source-grounding gate and cannot proceed. Two reviewers disagree about whether the missing-information section is easy to act on; their notes show the criterion needs a definition of owner and next action. I revise the rubric before the next round.

## Action checklist

- Define the output or run unit that each score represents.
- Choose a simple scale and write what each level means for every criterion.
- Record evidence or a secure reference beside every score.
- Treat non-negotiable gates separately from average or total scores.
- Calibrate at least two reviewers on safe cases when appropriate.
- Log criterion changes and repeated scoring patterns before revising the system.

# Assign review and decision ownership

**I make governance operational by assigning a named owner to run, review, decide, escalate, change, and retire the system rather than leaving responsibility with “the team.”**

I clarify who has the authority and competence for each decision so the workflow does not drift when a case is unusual, a person is absent, or a change seems urgent.

I have seen many workable systems become fragile because their responsibilities were implied. One person thinks the operator checks the source; the operator assumes the manager approves it; the manager expects a technical owner to notice a prompt change. When something goes wrong, everyone can describe the workflow, but nobody can say who had authority to stop it. I prevent that by making the roles visible before I expand use.

I begin with the lifecycle, not only the live run. A system needs an operational owner who keeps the current instructions and access path usable; an operator who initiates the defined work; a reviewer who checks the output and evidence; a decision owner who authorizes the consequential next action; an escalation owner for exceptions; a change owner who approves revisions; and, where relevant, a data, privacy, security, safety, legal, or domain owner. One person can perform multiple roles in a small operation, but I preserve the distinctions so the conflicts and gaps are visible.

For each role, I write a decision statement. The operator may run only approved inputs in the current version and must pause on defined signals. The reviewer may mark a draft pass, revise, or stop based on the rubric but may not approve an out-of-scope commitment. The decision owner may accept or reject the final next action within their authority. The change owner may approve a revised prompt after the required test evidence exists. Clear statements are better than job titles alone because titles vary across organizations.

I identify delegated authority and absence coverage. If the usual reviewer is unavailable, who can take over? If no authorized person is available, does the case wait, use a manual path, or stop? I do not solve a coverage gap by giving broad decision rights to the AI or a junior operator. A fallback owner must be appropriately qualified for the decision they may make. This is especially important for health, law, employment, compensation, finance, housing, benefits, education access, safety, discipline, and essential services.

I also separate advice from accountability. A community member, tool vendor, or external consultant may offer useful feedback, but they do not become the decision owner for a member’s live case. The person or organization with the relevant authority remains accountable for the evidence, final determination, and communication. I make this boundary explicit when I ask for critique so nobody mistakes a template or score for professional approval.

Change ownership needs checks and balances. If the same person can change a prompt, decide that the change passed, and deploy it to a consequential workflow without review, I have a weak governance path. The right separation depends on scale, but I at least require a recorded test and a designated approver for behavior-affecting changes. I include emergency changes too: who can disable a system immediately, how they record it, and who reviews the restoration.

I make the ownership map accessible from the system home and failure log. During ordinary work, the operator should see who reviews the output. During an exception, they should see where to send it. During a change, the proposer should know what evidence the approver needs. A beautiful governance document stored far from the workflow is less useful than a concise role map next to the runbook.

Finally, I review ownership after real use. New volume, a new data category, a different user group, or repeated review delays may mean a role needs more capacity or a different authority boundary. I record the finding, change the map through the approved process, and tell operators what changed. Governance is a maintained operating practice, not a one-time signature.

## Concrete example

I assign ownership for an AI-assisted research brief used by a product team. A researcher runs the current workflow on approved sources. A product lead reviews relevance and source grounding. A compliance owner handles privacy or regulated claims. The product manager decides whether a brief informs a roadmap discussion; the system cannot make that decision. The operations lead approves prompt changes after a safe retest, and a designated backup covers the product lead’s review role during leave.

## Action checklist

- List the operational, operator, reviewer, decision, escalation, change, and retirement roles.
- Write the authority and limit of each role in a one-sentence decision statement.
- Identify qualified backup coverage and the action when no owner is available.
- Separate advisory feedback from final decision accountability.
- Define approval and emergency-disable authority for behavior-affecting changes.
- Place the current ownership map beside the runbook and escalation path.

## Required deliverable

Create a **Review and Decision Ownership Map** in the Governance and Permissions Checklist. Include named roles or accountable functions, authority limits, backup coverage, conflict or separation needs, change approval, emergency-stop authority, and high-impact human decision owners.

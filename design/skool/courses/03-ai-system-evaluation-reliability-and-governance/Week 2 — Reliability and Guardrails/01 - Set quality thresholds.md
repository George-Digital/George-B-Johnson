# Set quality thresholds

**I set thresholds before I review results so the system has a clear, proportionate rule for when to proceed, revise, sample more closely, or pause.**

I turn scores and acceptance gates into operating decisions without pretending that one percentage proves a workflow is dependable in every future case.

A threshold is the line that changes what I do next. It is not a decorative target and it is not a license to remove human judgment. I set one because a collection of individual scores can otherwise produce a familiar problem: everyone agrees that some outputs are weak, but nobody has decided whether the workflow should be revised, restricted, or stopped. The threshold makes that decision visible before a favorable result creates pressure to expand too quickly.

I begin with the acceptance gates. Some conditions are not averaged: unauthorized data use, unsupported material claims, a prohibited decision, a missed escalation, or a failure to follow an essential safety or review boundary. A single critical gate failure can require containment and investigation even if nine other cases look strong. I write those gates separately from my quality score because a good average can hide a serious defect.

For non-gate criteria, I choose thresholds that fit the intended use and the amount of evidence I actually have. An early low-consequence draft workflow may require every evaluated case to pass source and privacy gates, plus a defined majority of other criteria, before I use it with close human review. A workflow that reaches many users, creates commitments, or influences consequential work deserves tighter thresholds, more representative coverage, and more frequent review. I do not borrow a percentage from another team or an online benchmark without understanding its use case, data, and decision boundary.

I also distinguish case-level and set-level rules. A case-level rule tells the operator what to do with one output: accept for review, revise, request information, route, or stop. A set-level rule tells the owner what to do after a group of cases: keep the current scope, run another evaluation round, change the workflow, reduce the intended use, or pause operation. I need both. A single incomplete request may be handled correctly by asking for more information, while a pattern of incomplete-request failures may show that the input design needs work.

I include uncertainty in my threshold decision. Six cases with five passes can be encouraging, but they are not a broad assurance. If I have few cases, inconsistent reviewers, a changing source, or a new configuration, I treat the result as limited evidence and retain stronger review. I state the limitation instead of announcing that the system is ready for every case. Reliability grows through observed evidence and controlled use, not through a single score.

I choose action bands that operators can remember. For example: all gates pass and the set meets the quality rule means continue in the stated scope with review; gates pass but a criterion pattern falls below the target means revise and retest; a critical gate fails or the reviewer cannot verify the output means stop the case and escalate. The exact labels can differ, but the action must be clear. I make sure a busy operator does not need to interpret a chart while handling a live exception.

Thresholds need owners and review dates. The person who owns operations may apply the rule, but a domain, privacy, safety, or compliance owner may need to approve a change when the threshold protects their area. I record who can alter the threshold and what evidence they require. I do not let a short-term desire for speed lower a privacy or high-impact decision boundary without authorized review.

Finally, I test the threshold against actual safe cases. If a clearly unsafe output can pass, the threshold is too loose. If a useful, well-supported draft always triggers a stop for a nonessential preference, it may be too rigid. I adjust with evidence, record the reason, and keep the previous version. A threshold is an operating control that should be inspectable and revisable—not an arbitrary score that everyone learns to work around.

## Concrete example

I set thresholds for an internal project-intake brief. Every case must pass source grounding, privacy, and correct-routing gates. A case may enter manager review when at least four of five non-gate criteria pass; otherwise the operator revises or requests missing information. If two of eight recent cases miss the same required field, the process owner pauses expansion, investigates the input template, and reruns the affected cases. The brief never approves budget or staffing decisions.

## Action checklist

- List the critical gates that cannot be offset by other scores.
- Define a case-level accept, revise, route, or stop action for each result band.
- Define a set-level continue, investigate, restrict, or pause action.
- Match threshold strictness to consequence, volume, uncertainty, and reversibility.
- Name who applies, approves changes to, and reviews the thresholds.
- Test the rule against one strong and one clearly unacceptable safe result.

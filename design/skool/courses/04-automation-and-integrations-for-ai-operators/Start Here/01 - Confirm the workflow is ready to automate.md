# Confirm the workflow is ready to automate

**I automate a step only after I can show that the manual version is stable, reviewable, and worth repeating.**

Before I connect anything, I make a clear readiness decision about one bounded workflow.

I treat automation as a change to an operating process, not as a reward for having a clever idea. A workflow is ready for a pilot when I can describe its common path without inventing steps while I run it. I need a repeated trigger, a useful output, known inputs, a named owner, and a human who can judge whether the output is acceptable. If I cannot point to those things, I still have design work to do.

I look for evidence from real manual runs. I want to know whether the same sequence succeeds often enough to be worth repeating, where people make judgment calls, how often exceptions appear, and what happens when an input is incomplete. A workflow that feels repetitive may still conceal important variation. I do not solve that uncertainty by automating the variation. I name it and keep it with the person responsible for the decision.

I also separate a stable task from a stable outcome. A team may reliably receive a request, but the correct response may depend on a current policy, a private source, or a high-impact judgment. In that case, I may automate intake, formatting, routing, or a draft, while preserving human review of the consequential part. I do not let an automated handoff imply authority it does not have.

Readiness includes a practical recovery question: can an operator complete the work manually if the connection is unavailable, data is wrong, or a review is delayed? If the answer is no, I have made the process more fragile, not more useful. I document the manual path before I pilot the automated one, because that path is both a safety boundary and a source of comparison.

I am careful with access as well. The convenience of connecting a source does not establish permission to send, retain, or transform its information. I identify the approved data, the people allowed to operate the workflow, and the decisions that remain with a qualified human. For sensitive, regulated, financial, health, employment, legal, safety, or access-related work, I keep the automated output advisory or administrative unless an authorized owner has defined a review and escalation process.

Finally, I make a narrow go or no-go statement. “Automate our operations” is not a decision. “Pilot a structured intake-to-draft brief for complete internal requests, with an operator approval before handoff” is a decision I can inspect. A no-go is useful too. It tells me to improve the manual baseline, inputs, or review arrangement before I add a connection.

## Concrete example

I might see a team manually turn complete event requests into a planning brief several times a week. The request form has required fields, the coordinator follows the same sequence, and a lead already reviews venue and safety details. I can consider a pilot that creates a draft brief and flags missing information. I do not automate approval of event safety, spending, or accessibility choices. If the form is incomplete or the lead is unavailable, the request stays in the manual queue.

## Action checklist

- Gather three recent, safe examples of the manual workflow.
- Name the trigger, common-path output, operator, reviewer, and final owner.
- List the decisions that must remain human decisions.
- Describe the manual fallback in one or two sentences.
- Write a pilot boundary that excludes unusual or sensitive cases.

## Required deliverable

Complete the Automation Readiness Scorecard for one workflow and record a provisional decision: ready for a limited pilot, not ready, or ready only after a named condition is met.

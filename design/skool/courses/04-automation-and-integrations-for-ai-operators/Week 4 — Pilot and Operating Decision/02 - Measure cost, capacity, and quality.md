# Measure cost, capacity, and quality

**I compare the pilot with the manual baseline using evidence that includes human effort, errors, review load, and output quality.**

I measure whether the bounded process is practical to operate, not merely whether it can produce an output.

I do not judge a pilot by speed alone. An automated step can reduce one person’s touch time while increasing review load, creating more correction work, or adding an access and maintenance burden. I compare the pilot to the manual baseline using measures that match the actual user and decision.

I begin with volume and capacity. How many eligible cases arrived? How many completed the common path? How many paused, required manual handling, or waited for review? I compare this with the capacity of the operator and reviewer. A workflow that handles more cases than the team can inspect is not necessarily helping; it may be producing a faster backlog.

I measure time in context. I note operator touch time, reviewer time, wait time, and recovery time for exceptions. I do not assume every minute saved is a net benefit if the task now requires configuration, monitoring, training, or incident response. I write down the maintenance activities that exist because of the pilot and who performed them.

Quality remains a gate. I use the output contract and rubric to inspect completeness, factual grounding against approved sources, usability for the next action, boundary adherence, and domain-specific criteria. I report returns, corrections, unsupported claims, missing fields, and cases that should not have entered the pilot. A polished output that crosses a scope or privacy boundary is not a quality success.

I track cost carefully and honestly. I may record direct usage or service charges, staff time, review time, storage or access cost, and the cost of rework. Early numbers are estimates and may vary with volume, model behavior, source changes, or team practice. I state the assumptions instead of projecting a broad financial outcome from a small sample.

I look for distributions, not just averages. One difficult case, long review, or repeated error may matter more than the average when it reveals a risk the design cannot handle. I separate common-path results from excluded cases so I do not make the pilot look stronger by ignoring its manual routes.

At the end, I create a short operating picture: what the workflow handled, how it performed against the baseline, what people still had to do, what failed, what it cost to run and maintain, and what evidence is still missing. That picture supports a responsible launch, revision, or pause decision.

## Concrete example

Over two weeks, a pilot accepts twenty complete internal requests. Fourteen produce drafts that pass review with minor edits, three need substantial revision, and three route to manual handling because inputs are incomplete. The coordinator spends less time on formatting but the lead spends extra time checking source references. Direct usage cost is modest, but the team also records setup time and daily monitoring. The evidence suggests the pilot is useful only if the intake form improves and the source-reference check is made easier; it does not justify expanding to all request types yet.

## Action checklist

- Compare eligible volume, completion, pause, manual-route, and review counts with the baseline.
- Record operator, reviewer, wait, recovery, and maintenance time.
- Score outputs against quality, source, scope, and privacy gates.
- Record direct and staff costs with the assumptions behind each estimate.
- Separate common-path evidence from exclusions and unresolved risks.

## Required deliverable

Create a pilot measurement summary with baseline comparison, volume, capacity, quality results, exceptions, operator and reviewer effort, costs, assumptions, and evidence still needed.

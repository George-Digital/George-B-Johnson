# Decide whether to launch, revise, or pause

**I make the next operating decision from pilot evidence, keeping the scope narrow when quality, ownership, or recovery is not yet sufficient.**

I treat launch, revision, and pause as responsible outcomes rather than forcing every pilot toward expansion.

At the end of a pilot, I do not ask whether the workflow is exciting. I ask whether the evidence supports a specific operating decision. The available choices are usually narrower than people expect: continue a defined pilot, launch the current bounded path, revise and retest, narrow the eligible cases, or pause and return to the manual baseline. Each can be the right result.

I begin with gates, not averages. Did the workflow respect the input, source, privacy, access, and approval boundaries? Did consequential actions remain with the authorized human? Could operators detect errors and use the manual fallback? A low cost or fast common path does not outweigh a gate failure. When a gate fails, I pause or revise the affected path before considering broader use.

Next I review quality and capacity. I compare output results to the documented rubric, not to a vague impression. I look at correction rate, review time, backlog, manual routes, and operator experience. I ask whether the current team can sustain the review and maintenance load at the proposed scope. A process that requires constant builder intervention is not ready for routine operation.

I inspect the evidence limits. A small sample, short time period, synthetic cases, or one type of request may be enough for a constrained decision but not for a general claim. I state what the pilot tested and what it did not. If a source, policy, tool behavior, or ownership arrangement changes, I may need another dry run even if the previous pilot was acceptable.

For a launch decision, I document the exact scope, start date, owners, review rule, monitoring period, manual fallback, and conditions that trigger a pause. I do not write “fully automated” when humans still review, resolve exceptions, maintain sources, and authorize decisions. The operating description should match reality.

For a revise decision, I identify the smallest change and retest case. For a pause decision, I preserve the evidence, communicate the manual path, revoke or restrict access as appropriate through the approved process, and name what would need to change before reconsideration. Pausing is not abandoning the work; it is refusing to normalize an unsupported risk.

I share the decision with the people who operate and own the workflow. They need to know what changes tomorrow, what stays manual, where the current handoff lives, and how to report a problem. Clear communication prevents the pilot from becoming a shadow process that different people understand differently.

A responsible operator can show the reasoning behind the decision. That record makes future improvement easier and keeps the course outcome honest: a controlled recurring process only where the evidence and boundaries support one.

## Concrete example

A team reviews a request-to-brief pilot. Quality passes for complete, low-risk requests, but source references take too long for the reviewer and incomplete requests still cause frequent manual routes. The team does not expand. It revises the intake form, adds a more visible source-reference field, and continues only with the original request type for another measured period. The operating decision names the project lead as reviewer, the coordinator as operator, a manual template for failures, and a pause trigger if a source or access boundary changes.

## Action checklist

- Review all gate failures before weighing speed, volume, or cost.
- Compare quality, capacity, review burden, recovery evidence, and maintenance ownership to the baseline.
- State the tested scope and the limits of the evidence.
- Choose launch, continue pilot, revise, narrow, or pause with a written reason.
- Document owners, monitoring, manual fallback, and the next evidence threshold.
